Case study two — Design systems

Redesigning the patient drawer into a modular service management system

APODIENSTE, 2024 — PharmaBits

Figma / FigJam / Notion / Figma Make / Maze

Product designer — discovery and research, iterating on design concepts, co-creating with stakeholders, designing user flows and journey maps, conducting usability tests, and contributing to the completion of the design library and design system.

Context

apodienste helps pharmacies manage services such as polymedication analysis, blood pressure monitoring and inhalation training. The platform was originally built to help call centres handle patient appointments, but the pharmacists who carried out the services found it hard to use.

The need for a more intuitive and scalable solution became apparent, prompting a redesign to better serve both call centres and pharmacies.

Design credit
Moein Fakhravar — design team lead
Team
Product design team lead, engineers, stakeholders
Timeline
March 2024 – design handoff (July 2024) – release (September 2024)

As a product designer, I was responsible for discovery and research, iterating on design concepts, co-creating with stakeholders, designing user flows and journey maps, conducting usability tests, and contributing to the completion of the design library and design system.

Challenges

  1. Confusing mental model. Pharmacists struggled to relate to the concept of "cases" and found it difficult to understand why patient documents and case documents were in different places within the drawer. This confusion hindered their ability to execute tasks efficiently.
  2. High cognitive load. The system displayed too much information, much of which was unnecessary, overwhelming pharmacists with irrelevant data and complicating the service delivery process.
  3. Rigid and unscalable. Each new service required structural UI changes, making it difficult to scale the system as new services were added.
  4. Manual navigation burden. Pharmacists had to manually search for documents rather than being guided through a clear, streamlined service execution path. The process didn't align with the fast-paced, business-driven environment of the pharmacy.

"How might we improve the pharmacy service workflow in a scalable and intuitive way that accommodates the needs of multiple user groups — such as call centre agents and pharmacists — while ensuring efficiency, clarity and ease of use across different service types?"

Problem statement

Constraints

  1. Direct access to users, especially pharmacists, was challenging, making large-scale interviews and continuous feedback loops difficult.
  2. As this was a unique and comprehensive feature, there were no existing industry patterns or references to benchmark against.
  3. Existing users were already familiar with the old design, so the new solution needed to minimise the onboarding curve.
  4. The new design had to align with the existing backend structure, allowing only minimal changes.

Process

  1. Research & discovery. Conducted stakeholder interviews, gathered feedback from early pharmacy adopters, and analysed call centre workflows and diary studies to understand user needs and operational challenges.
  2. Problem definition. Synthesised research findings to identify core pain points and defined design goals focused on usability and flexibility.
  3. Ideation & concept development. Explored multiple design directions, including pDL-based cases, documentation modals and service-based workflows, before converging on a modular approach that balanced scalability with clarity.
  4. Iteration & design refinement. Developed low- to high-fidelity prototypes, ran usability tests with pharmacists and call centre users, and refined the design based on feedback to ensure intuitive workflows.
  5. Technical exploration. Worked closely with the engineering team across several refinement and design crit sessions to align the new design with the backend structures, minimising required technical changes.
  6. Handoff, onboarding & implementation support. Delivered the final design in two sprints and collaborated with the development team during implementation. Created a detailed Notion-based onboarding document and a Synthesia video tutorial to guide users step by step through the new modular workflow.

Research insights — different roles, different needs

Each user group — call centre, analyst and pharmacist — works in a distinct environment with unique objectives and time pressures. A single linear workflow didn't fit all.

"How can I see what the pharmacist has done? I don't even know when or how a service gets closed."

Call centre user

"How can I be sure that the patient has actually been contacted and still wants the service so I can start the analysis?"

Analyst

"I can't tell where to find the right documents for each patient — and I'm not even sure what a 'case' is supposed to mean here."

Pharmacist

Fig. 3–5 — role-based personas and their needs, based on the interview and research.

  1. Pharmacists needed a guided, step-based flow for in-session tasks and document signing.
  2. Call centre users required clarity and feedback after creating services and appointments.
  3. Analysts needed a structured overview to prepare medication data before sessions.
  4. The existing "case" model caused confusion and unnecessary complexity.
  5. The system needed to be scalable and require minimal retraining for existing users.

Concept development & iterations

Continuous iterations.

After identifying that different user groups (call centre, analyst, pharmacist) had very different needs, the goal was to find a simpler and more intuitive way for everyone to manage patient services. The challenge was to make the process clear and flexible — whether a user was booking an appointment, analysing medication data, or meeting the patient in person. Several ideas and prototypes were explored:

Iteration 1. pDL-based case

The first idea was to tie each "case" to one service, such as blood pressure or medication review. While this gave structure, users found it confusing — especially when a patient needed multiple services.

"The Case concept is not understandable for the pharmacy user. Besides, the user does not know if blood pressure/inhalation is needed for the patient or not before starting the process."

Issue and feedback

Iteration 2. Documentation modal

Documentation was separated into its own window to make signing and uploading forms easier.

"Case and documents are related and we'd better not to separate them, this looks only like a temporary solution to the problem. It's not expandable and flexible."

Issue and feedback

Iteration 3. Separating the documentation tab

The idea was to define different steps of a service and optimise documentation based on the current state of the case, so the pharmacist only needed to work with the documentation tab.

"The cognitive load is very high, users struggle to understand the process especially after a number of services, it's also not scalable."

Issue and feedback

Iteration 4. Service modules

The goal was to make every part of the process — appointments, documents and comments — function as individual modules that sit together within the same interface, giving users flexibility to perform any action at any point in the service flow.

"The layout feel unstructured and lacking workflow hierarchy. It became clear that the modular concept needed better grouping and clearer guidance to support a natural service flow."

Issue and feedback

Eureka!

By viewing each step — appointments, documents and comments — as modular building blocks rather than fixed sections, we unlocked a way to make the system both flexible and structured. This shift in perspective transformed the entire design approach and became the foundation for the final, scalable solution.

The iterations continued…

Design decision

The final design brought together everything learned from earlier iterations — combining the clarity of a guided flow with the flexibility of a modular system. Each part of the process (appointment, documentation, signing and comments) became a module within one unified drawer, allowing users to complete or revisit any step without losing context.

Usability testing & research plan

Once the modular design reached a stable version, scenario-based usability tests were conducted with both pharmacists and call centre users to evaluate how intuitive the new flow felt in practice. Participants were asked to perform key tasks such as creating a service, setting an appointment, reviewing documentation, and completing a patient session.

While early insights were gathered through stakeholder feedback and initial user discussions, a structured research plan was created after developing the modular design. This allowed the new workflow to be validated with real users, measuring its impact both pre- and post-launch.

Key findings from pre-launch usability testing

  1. High task success. Participants completed 80% of the core tasks successfully on their first attempt, with minimal guidance.
  2. Positive first impressions. Users described the modular layout as "clear," "modern," and "much easier to follow."
  3. Archive discoverability. Several users had difficulty finding and understanding the document and service archives, suggesting the need for clearer labelling and grouping.
  4. Appointment flow. The appointment module required refinement — users were unsure if appointments were saved or linked to the correct service.
  5. Progress clarity. While helpful, the progress indicator needed stronger visual contrast and clearer step descriptions to better communicate service status.

Implementation & handoff

After validating the modular concept through usability testing, the team collaborated closely with engineering to bring the design to production. Over four grooming sessions, interaction details, API dependencies and system behaviours were refined to ensure smooth integration with the existing backend. The design was handed off in two sprints, complete with detailed Figma specifications, component documentation and a clear roadmap for incremental rollout.

Onboarding & user guidance

To support user adoption and onboarding, two resources were created: a Notion-based onboarding guide outlining every step of the service workflow, and a Synthesia video tutorial demonstrating real scenarios for pharmacists and call centre users.

Outcome & impact

  1. 30% faster task completion during patient sessions.
  2. Significant reduction in process-related support tickets.
  3. Positive adoption feedback from pilot pharmacies, describing the new flow as "logical," "well-organized," and "finally practical for daily use."
  4. Scalability achieved. New services can now be added as independent modules without major UI or backend changes.
