Case study one — Workflow design

Rebuilding a workflow builder around the people who use it most

ARKK, 2026 — Enterprise data platform

Canvas, activity configuration and in-context testing for an enterprise tax platform.

Overview

Product
ARKK — enterprise data platform for tax and finance teams
Timeframe
April – July 2026
My role
Product designer — owned two of three initiatives end to end
Team
Engineering lead, PM, and the designer who ran discovery
Scope
Three initiatives: canvas, activity drawer, workflow testing
Platform
Web, desktop
Tools
Figma, FigJam

The workflow canvas is the core of the product, but users couldn't zoom or pan it, the activity drawer didn't scale, and there was no way to test a workflow without leaving the environment. Across three initiatives I converged the canvas redesign, designed a configuration drawer built to hold activity types that don't exist yet, and scoped an MVP for testing in place. The work is specced and awaiting build.

The people building workflows are the ones with the least time

Workflows are built by ARKK's platform solutions team — tax accountants and specialists whose value is working out how a client's tax process should be solved. Time spent fighting the tool is time not spent on that.

The roadmap points somewhere harder: clients' own tax teams are eventually meant to build workflows without a specialist in the middle. Anything designed for today's expert user has to hold up for a less practised one later.

The challenge

Figure — the legacy modal-based editor
(source screenshot to be supplied)

Three problems, established through user and stakeholder interviews run during discovery before I joined:

  1. No basic canvas controls. No zoom, no pan. Anything beyond a small workflow became hard to navigate.
  2. The drawer didn't scale. Fields sat in a single flat, scrolling stack — activities had no grouping or ordering, so complex configurations were hard to navigate.
  3. Validation happened elsewhere. To find out whether a workflow ran, users scheduled it and left the workflow environment, then returned for the result. Checking the work meant abandoning it.

Where I joined the process

Programme-level discovery was run by another designer before I joined, along with the divergence phase of the canvas initiative, which had already settled on Foblex Flow as the foundation.

I picked the canvas up at convergence: the direction was set and confidence was high on what the product needed to do, so the work was to decide what the redesign actually became inside a library decision already made.

The activity drawer and workflow testing initiatives were mine end to end — divergence and convergence both — designed against the inherited discovery findings rather than research I ran myself.

What Foblex Flow gave, and what it cost

Foblex Flow — the third-party Angular library engineering had already chosen — handled the canvas mechanics. Everything past that, the parts that made it feel like a considered product rather than a library demo, needed custom design.

Out of the box

  1. Node dragging and repositioning.
  2. Single-node selection.
  3. Basic viewport controls — zoom, pan, minimap.
  4. Fixed connection ports and auto-routed connector lines.

Needed custom design

  1. Node states for status, error and publish.
  2. Connection styling that reflected selection state.
  3. Multi-select, grouping and collapsible nodes.
  4. Search and filter across activities, and a contextual canvas toolbar.
  5. The activity configuration drawer itself.

Initiative one — the canvas

Converging meant deciding what the canvas actually became inside Foblex Flow's constraints: custom node designs carrying status, error and publish states; connection styling that reflected selection; and a canvas toolbar for search, zoom, grouping and fit-to-viewport, layered on top of the library's baseline. Several full-page layout concepts were explored before settling on a full-screen canvas with a configurable side panel — the shape the rest of the case study builds on.

Initiative two — designing a container, not a set of screens

The framing decision was scope: not to design each activity type, but to design a container that could hold any of them, including ones that didn't exist yet. A drawer built around the current set would need redesigning every time the product grew.

The drawer that came out of that decision uses a fixed header for name and description, a scrollable body for settings, and a footer for contextual actions — the old flat stack redesigned into a shape built to extend to activity types that don't exist yet.

Why tabs

Two reasons decided it, both from the content itself rather than a preference for tabs as a pattern:

  1. The sections weren't sequential. Both had to be filled and neither came first. A stepper would have implied an order that didn't exist.
  2. A single stack would have run long. One scrolling column meant endless scroll on denser activities — the exact failure the old drawer already had.

Layouts considered

Tabbed — shipped

  • Kept fields off-screen until needed
  • Gave nested fields nowhere to go

Accordion

  • Scaled well; sections stayed visible collapsed
  • Long sections felt overwhelming

Drill-down menu

  • Kept the main view uncluttered
  • Too many clicks to reach related fields

The size I didn't think we needed

I planned for two: a third of the screen for lighter configuration, full screen for dense ones.

A stakeholder insisted on a half-screen size as well, for a behaviour I hadn't accounted for — users move back and forth between the workflow and the activity they're configuring, checking one against the other. At a third the drawer is too cramped for dense settings; at full screen the workflow disappears. Half screen keeps both readable.

The drawer shipped with three sizes. The third exists because someone closer to the users than I was said so, and was right.

Initiative three — closing the validation loop

I designed an MVP for testing in place, so an activity could be validated without scheduling it and navigating away.

What it deliberately left out

No diff, and no data pulling for activities — both high enough effort to have swallowed the initiative. The MVP answers a narrower question than the eventual feature will: whether an activity runs, not what it produced or what changed. That was the trade to close the loop at all in the time available.

What I deferred

Undo and redo on the canvas is a real gap and users feel it, but the complexity of doing it properly sat outside what three initiatives could absorb. Solving it badly inside a workflow editor is worse than not solving it yet, so it was set aside to be designed on its own.

Where it stands, and what I'd measure

Three initiatives delivered a redesigned canvas, an activity configuration drawer in three sizes, and an MVP for in-context workflow testing. The work is specced and sits with engineering; release cycles on the platform are long, and it hasn't been built yet.

Once it ships I'd want to know:

  1. How long it takes to build a workflow.
  2. How often users leave the environment to validate one.
  3. Whether a new activity type can be added to the drawer without redesigning it — the direct test of the container decision.

What I'd do differently

Designing the activity drawer and workflow testing against inherited discovery findings, rather than research I ran myself, cost precision at least once: the half-screen drawer size existed because a stakeholder caught a real usage pattern the original discovery hadn't surfaced, not because I had. Next time, on a scope I didn't originate, I'd want direct contact with users earlier — not just the findings handed to me.
